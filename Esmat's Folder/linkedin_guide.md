# LinkedIn optimisation guide for Code Institute Data Analytics with AI graduates

Use this guide as a build manual. Work through the phases in order, save changes as you go, and check the final profile as a recruiter or hiring manager might. LinkedIn changes its interface, so button labels can vary slightly by device or account.

The aim is to make your target role, relevant experience and evidence easy to understand. A stronger profile can help people assess your fit; it cannot guarantee search visibility, an interview or a job.

## Phase 1: Identity and settings

### Step 1: Set your target and location

Decide which entry-level roles you are targeting before editing the profile. Examples include:

- Data Analyst
- Junior Data Analyst
- Reporting Analyst
- Business Intelligence Analyst

Choose titles that match the work you want and the vacancies you are applying for. Check several current adverts, including roles in the West Midlands, and note the titles, tools and skills employers actually request. Job titles vary between organisations; do not assume every analyst role has the same requirements.

Set your LinkedIn location accurately. If you are open to suitable locations beyond your home town, use LinkedIn's job preferences to show the areas you would genuinely consider.

### Step 2: Review your profile visibility and job-search settings

1. Open your LinkedIn profile and review **Public profile & URL** or the equivalent profile visibility control.
2. Check which sections can be seen by people outside LinkedIn and in search engines. Use the public-profile preview if available.
3. If you want to use **Open to Work**, choose the audience and job preferences that suit your situation. LinkedIn's “Recruiters only” setting is not a guarantee of complete privacy from your current employer.
4. Before making a series of edits, check whether LinkedIn will notify your network. Turn off profile-update notifications if you do not want those changes announced.
5. Avoid publishing your home address, private contact details, confidential work information, or personal or employer data.

### Step 3: Add a profile photo and optional banner

Use a clear, current photo if you are comfortable having one on your profile. Choose a simple banner only if it supports your professional presentation. A banner is optional; neither the photo nor banner replaces evidence of your skills.

Use images you have permission to publish. Do not include private information, confidential charts or identifiable people without permission.

### Step 4: Write a focused headline

Your headline appears under your name and can be different from your current or previous job title. State your target honestly, then add a small number of skills you can demonstrate.

**Example**

> Data Analytics with AI Graduate | Aspiring Data Analyst | Python, Pandas and Data Visualisation

Adjust the tools to reflect what you have actually used. Do not call yourself an experienced data analyst if you have not held that role. Avoid keyword strings that are difficult to read. Recruiters may search using job titles, skills and locations, but LinkedIn does not publish the exact ranking weights for profile text.

## Phase 2: Narrative and experience

### Step 5: Build your About section

Use a few short paragraphs to answer:

1. What role are you targeting?
2. What relevant strengths did you develop in your earlier career?
3. What have you learned on the Code Institute programme?
4. What evidence can a reader review?
5. What kind of opportunity are you looking for?

Write in the first person and use specific examples. Explain the career change as a deliberate next step, not as an apology for your previous work.

**Example structure**

> I am a Code Institute Data Analytics with AI graduate seeking an entry-level data analyst role. My earlier experience in [field] developed my ability to [relevant, evidenced strengths].
>
> Through my studies, I have practised [tools and methods you have used] and applied them to [brief project or question]. I am particularly interested in using data to [business or service goal].
>
> I am looking for an entry-level opportunity in [location or sector, if relevant] where I can continue developing my analytical skills and help teams make informed decisions.

Replace every bracketed phrase with accurate details. Do not claim a qualification, tool, result or level of expertise you cannot explain.

### Step 6: Translate previous roles into transferable evidence

Keep your earlier roles and use their real titles. Do not rename a teaching, administration, retail, care or other non-data job as “Data Analyst”. Instead, select work that shows relevant behaviours and explain what you did, why it mattered and what changed.

For each role:

1. Start with the actual job title, employer and dates.
2. Choose one or two relevant examples rather than listing every duty.
3. Describe the action and context: what information, records, process or decision did you work with?
4. State the outcome only if it is true and safe to share.
5. Connect the example to a transferable skill, such as data quality, record-keeping, pattern recognition, problem-solving, stakeholder communication or evidence-based decisions.
6. Mention software only if you really used it, and do not overstate what you used it for.

**Fictional teaching example**

**Before**

> Taught lessons, marked work, kept attendance and assessment records, supported pupils and spoke with parents.

**After**

> Maintained attendance and assessment records and reviewed progress to identify pupils who might need additional support. Used Arbor to support data-informed interventions, communicated relevant findings to colleagues and families, and reviewed whether the support was helping.

This describes transferable experience; it does not claim that the person previously held a data analyst position. Use an example like the Arbor one only if it reflects your own experience. Never disclose pupil, client, patient, customer or employer-identifiable information.

## Phase 3: Evidence and education

### Step 7: Add your Code Institute education

Add your Code Institute Data Analytics with AI programme in **Education** so readers can see where you developed your technical skills.

1. Open your profile.
2. Select **Add profile section**.
3. Open **Core** and select **Add education** (wording or placement may vary).
4. Enter Code Institute as the institution and use the programme name shown on your course record.
5. Add accurate dates and any credential details that apply.
6. In the description, list the programme topics you studied and can discuss:
   - Python
   - Pandas
   - Numpy
   - ML feature-engine
   - Matplotlib
   - Seaborn
   - Plotly
7. Save, then check the entry on your profile.

Treat the topic list as evidence of study, not proof of mastery by itself. Be ready to describe how you used each topic in coursework or projects. If a topic does not match your completed syllabus or experience, verify it against your course materials and make the description accurate before publishing.

### Step 8: Add relevant skills and connect them to evidence

Choose skills based on your completed course, project work and target vacancies. Include technical skills you can explain, as well as a few relevant transferable skills. Prioritise accuracy and relevance over filling every available skill slot.

Where LinkedIn lets you associate a skill with a project, education entry or experience, connect it to the place where you used it. For example, connect Python or Pandas to a specific analysis project if you can describe your contribution. Do not rely on endorsements or assessments as substitutes for examples; LinkedIn says Skill Assessments are no longer available.

### Step 9: Prepare and Feature Project Evidence

Select one or two projects that best demonstrate your current ability. For each, prepare a short explanation covering:

- **Question:** What were you trying to find out?
- **Data:** Where did it come from, and is it appropriate to share?
- **Preparation:** What cleaning or transformation did you perform?
- **Method:** Which tools or analytical steps did you use?
- **Result:** What did you find?
- **Communication:** How did you present the result, such as a chart or dashboard?
- **Limitations:** What should a reader not conclude from the analysis?
- **Contribution:** What did you personally do?

**Featured is hidden by default if you have not added it to your profile.** To add it:

1. Go to your LinkedIn profile.
2. Click **Add profile section**.
3. Select the **Recommended** tab.
4. Click **Add featured**.
5. Add the project link.
6. Give the item a clear title.
7. Use the project framework above—Question, Data, Preparation, Method, Result, Communication, Limitations and Contribution—to write its description. Keep the description concise and accurate.
8. Save and review the result on your profile.

Use clear project names and descriptions. Make links accessible and test them while signed out or in a private browser window if possible. Remove credentials, private datasets, personal data and confidential employer or client information before publishing anything. Feature a small number of strong, relevant examples rather than a collection of unrelated links. If you do not yet have a suitable public sample, build one first rather than featuring unfinished or private work.

### Step 10: Add a professional LinkedIn URL

If available, customise your public LinkedIn URL to a simple variation of your name or professional identity. Check the URL after changing it and update any CV or portfolio links that use the old address. Remember that a custom URL does not itself control which profile sections are public; check visibility separately.

## Phase 4: Final verification

### Step 11: Review the profile as a recruiter might

Read the introduction, About, Experience, Education, Skills and Featured sections from top to bottom. A reader should be able to understand your target role, career transition and strongest evidence without having to infer them.

Check that:

- Your LinkedIn profile and CV use consistent job titles, employers and dates.
- Earlier work is described accurately and no past job is falsely presented as a data analyst role.
- Your Code Institute programme and technical topics are clear and accurate.
- Every project link works and explains your own contribution.
- Your profile is readable on a mobile screen as well as a desktop.
- Your public-profile preview shows only what you intend to share.
