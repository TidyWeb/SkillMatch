# LinkedIn profiles for career changers into data analysis: research findings

**Research checked:** 27 October 2026  
**Audience:** Code Institute data analytics graduates seeking entry-level roles in the West Midlands.

## How to read the evidence

- **LinkedIn documentation** describes platform features or LinkedIn's own advice. It is the best source for what a feature does, not independent proof that using it gets someone hired.
- **Recruiter experience** is an individual practitioner's account. It is useful but should not be treated as a universal description of every recruiter's search.
- **Career-changer account** is a person's own account of a transition. It can suggest ways to tell a credible story, but it does not establish that the same approach will work for everyone.
- **Common claim** means advice repeated by profile-writing pages without strong evidence here for its effect. Treat it as a hypothesis, not a platform rule.

LinkedIn help pages are the starting point below. Older pages are marked when they are more than two years old; where possible, the feature was checked against a more recent LinkedIn help page. Advice about profile visibility or search is not a promise of recruiter contact or employment.

## Top ten priorities, ranked

1. **Make the target role obvious in the headline.** Use a truthful target such as “Aspiring Data Analyst” or “Entry-Level Data Analyst”, then add a small number of substantiated tools or strengths. LinkedIn says the headline is editable, can differ from the current job title and appears in search results [1][2]. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation.** The headline editing page is five years old, but the current profile guide still describes an editable headline.

2. **Explain the transition in the About section.** In a few short paragraphs, connect the earlier career to the new direction: what experience carries over, what data skills have been learned, and what roles are sought. LinkedIn describes About as a place for mission, motivation and skills [1][4]. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation.**

3. **Keep earlier roles, but describe relevant work and outcomes rather than copying a long duty list.** Show where the person organised information, spotted patterns, checked accuracy, solved a problem or explained a finding. Do not relabel an old job as a data role. A UK recruitment site's advice makes a similar case for context and impact, but it is unbylined and undated [15]. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation and one limited recruiter-practice source.**

4. **Show evidence of doing analysis, not just interest in it.** Feature one or two well-explained course or personal projects: the question, data source, cleaning, method, result, visualisation and limitations. LinkedIn supports samples and external links in Featured [1][8]; two provider-published career-switcher stories describe practical projects and moving into UK data analyst roles [16][22]. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation and self-reported career-changer stories; the stories are promotional, not independently audited.**

5. **Curate skills to match entry-level vacancies and support each with evidence.** List tools actually used, alongside relevant transferable skills; connect skills to a project, course or role where possible. LinkedIn's current help page permits up to 100 skills and says a relevant list helps people understand strengths and match opportunities [7]. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation.** A separate LinkedIn Talent Solutions article says 50 skills, but its date could not be established from the retrieved page; use the newer Help figure and avoid treating the maximum as a target [21].

6. **Make location and job preferences useful to a recruiter.** Set an accurate location and, if appropriate, specify relevant roles and preferred locations in Open to Work. LinkedIn says those preferences can help a profile appear when recruiters search for suitable candidates [11]. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation.** It does not say this guarantees visibility or a job.

7. **Make the profile consistent and truthful across sections.** Use accurate dates, official job titles and education. Explain a market-facing title in the description rather than replacing the real one; attach a skill only where it was genuinely used. LinkedIn says Experience associations must accurately represent professional work with an organisation [6]. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation.**

8. **Use a clear, current profile photograph if comfortable doing so.** LinkedIn recommends a professional photo and claims members with one receive up to twice as many profile views [1][3]. The Help page does not provide the study method or show that more views lead to interviews. Treat that figure as LinkedIn's claim, not an independently verified hiring outcome. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation, with an unexamined platform statistic.**

9. **Choose a simple public profile URL and decide what should be public.** LinkedIn supports a customised URL, usually a variation of a name or professional brand [9][10]. Review the public-profile preview rather than assuming it mirrors the signed-in profile. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation.**

10. **Treat the banner as optional decoration, not a substitute for proof.** A simple visual connected to data or the West Midlands can make the profile look considered, but LinkedIn's page only says a cover image can make the story more visually appealing and help it stand out [5]. The research found no reliable evidence that a banner improves entry-level hiring outcomes. **Evidence: LinkedIn documentation for the feature; common claim for its hiring impact.**

## Tickable checklist by section

### Photo

- [ ] The image is current, clear and recognisably me.
- [ ] I am comfortable with its visibility; LinkedIn lets me change photo visibility [3].
- [ ] I have not treated a photo as a substitute for skills or project evidence.

### Banner

- [ ] The banner is optional and does not compete with the headline or profile photo.
- [ ] Any image, chart or map is mine to use and contains no confidential or personal data.

### Headline

- [ ] A reader can tell that I am targeting an entry-level data analyst role.
- [ ] I name only tools and skills I can discuss with examples.
- [ ] I use readable wording rather than a string of repeated keywords.
- [ ] The headline is not misleading about my current or previous job title.

### About

- [ ] I explain the career change positively, without apologising for my previous career.
- [ ] I connect earlier experience to relevant work: for example, checking records, finding patterns, explaining results or making evidence-based decisions.
- [ ] I identify the data tools or methods I have actually practised.
- [ ] I say what kind of role or problem I am interested in.
- [ ] The text is concise and easy to scan; LinkedIn suggests one or two paragraphs [1].

### Experience

- [ ] Job titles, organisations and dates are accurate.
- [ ] Each relevant role has a short description of the work and its impact, not only a list of duties.
- [ ] Transferable skills are tied to an actual example.
- [ ] Data analysis is not implied where it did not happen.
- [ ] Course projects are labelled as projects or education, not paid employment.
- [ ] Any quantified outcome is true, explainable and not confidential.

### Skills

- [ ] The most relevant skills match real entry-level vacancies I am considering.
- [ ] I list only skills I can evidence and explain.
- [ ] I connect technical skills to the project, course or experience where I used them.
- [ ] I have not added skills merely to reach LinkedIn's limit.
- [ ] I am not relying on LinkedIn Skill Assessments: LinkedIn says they are no longer available [17].

### Featured

- [ ] I have selected a small number of relevant samples, if I have suitable work to show.
- [ ] Each project link opens and explains the question, data, method, result and limitations.
- [ ] The sample demonstrates my own contribution.
- [ ] Public project files contain no private, employer, client or identifiable personal data.
- [ ] I checked that LinkedIn still offers Featured; its help page describing the section is over two years old, while the current profile guide continues to support work samples and portfolio links [1][8].

### URL

- [ ] The public URL is readable and professional.
- [ ] I know that the public URL and public-profile visibility are separate settings [9][10].

### Privacy

- [ ] I reviewed LinkedIn's public-profile settings and the public preview, including search-engine visibility [9].
- [ ] I checked which profile changes may notify my network before editing [1][6].
- [ ] If I use Open to Work, I chose the intended audience. “Recruiters only” is not a guarantee of complete privacy, including from my current employer [11].
- [ ] I have not published a personal phone number, home address, private email, confidential work details or sensitive data unnecessarily.
- [ ] I verify a job and recruiter independently before sending identity or financial information, clicking unexpected links or paying fees. LinkedIn profile signals alone do not authenticate an offer.

## Before and after: earlier career translated into data-relevant evidence

**Fictional example — Maya, a former teacher.** The wording below is illustrative, not a claim about a real person's history. Any tools or outcomes should be included only if they are true for the person using the example.

**Before — long, duty-led version**

> I worked as a primary school teacher for several years. I planned lessons, marked work, kept attendance and assessment records, spoke with pupils and parents, attended staff meetings, supported pupils who needed extra help and prepared reports for the end of each term.

**After — concise, evidence-led version**

> **Primary School Teacher**  
> Maintained attendance and assessment records, reviewed pupil progress over time and used the findings to identify where additional support was needed. Explained progress clearly to colleagues and families, then reviewed whether support was helping.  
> **Relevant strengths:** accurate record-keeping, pattern recognition, communicating findings and evaluating interventions.  
> **Tools:** add Excel or another tool only if Maya actually used it for this work.

This keeps the earlier role visible and translates its relevant work without claiming that teaching was a data analyst job. A separate transition sentence in About could say: “I am moving into data analysis after experience using evidence to understand progress and explain findings. I am building practical skills in [tools actually studied] and am looking for an entry-level role where I can help teams make clearer decisions.”

## How recruiters search: known versus guessed

### Known from LinkedIn's own material

- LinkedIn Recruiter is a product for sourcing candidates; LinkedIn describes advanced search filters, recommended matches and AI-assisted search [13].
- A member can see Search Appearances, including job titles they were found for and some information about searchers [12]. This shows that LinkedIn exposes appearance insights; it does not explain the ranking formula.
- LinkedIn says specifying job types and preferred locations in Open to Work can help a profile appear in relevant recruiter searches [11].
- Current LinkedIn help says skills may be attached to positions, projects or education, and that relevant profile skills help others understand strengths and match opportunities [6][7][17].

### Recruiter-reported, but not universal

An unbylined UK recruitment-site article presents its author's first-person recruiter account: searching with Boolean queries and combinations of job titles, skills and locations, with salary sometimes included [15]. It also recommends using role language naturally in headline, About and Experience. The page has no visible author or publication date in the retrieved copy, so this is a useful practitioner account, not independently verified evidence that all LinkedIn recruiters search this way or that the headline has a special ranking weight.

### Guessed or not established by the sources

- The exact weight LinkedIn gives to the headline, About text, skill endorsements, profile completeness, activity or Open to Work.
- Whether a particular keyword repeated in several sections changes ranking, or whether “keyword optimisation” directly causes more recruiter messages.
- Whether every recruiter uses LinkedIn Recruiter, Boolean search, the same filters or the same job-title vocabulary.
- Whether a profile view, a search appearance or a recruiter connection request predicts an interview.

Use the vocabulary found in real job adverts for the roles being targeted, but write naturally and truthfully. LinkedIn's public product pages do not disclose the full search or recommendation algorithm.

## Linking LinkedIn and GitHub; privacy and job scams

LinkedIn Featured supports links to external websites and work samples [8]. For a data graduate, a LinkedIn link to a selected GitHub repository can make a project easier to reach. A useful repository should explain the question, dataset provenance, cleaning, analysis, visualisation, conclusions and limitations. GitHub also supports a public profile README in a public repository named after the user's account [18].

Link only work that is genuinely public and suitable for an employer to inspect. Do not upload workplace or client datasets, identifiable records, private coursework, credentials, API keys or personal information. A public GitHub repository is not a private portfolio. Use synthetic or properly anonymised data where the dataset licence and context permit it, and explain its source.

LinkedIn's public profile is a simplified profile that may appear to non-members and in search engines; members can control eligible sections [9]. Open to Work has a “Recruiters only” choice, but LinkedIn explicitly says it cannot guarantee complete privacy from a current employer [11]. “Recruiters only” should therefore be treated as a visibility preference, not a confidentiality promise.

This research did not retrieve a current LinkedIn-specific job-scam page. UK worker-protection initiative JobsAware advises that sharing too much personal information without understanding why it is needed can expose jobseekers to employment fraud; it describes losses of money or identifiable data without benefit [20]. The National Cyber Security Centre also warns that online personal information can make phishing more convincing and recommends reviewing social-media privacy settings [19]. Verify a vacancy on the employer's independently located careers site and use independently confirmed contact details before sharing sensitive information; do not rely on a convincing LinkedIn profile or badge alone. These are general safety precautions, not a claim that every unsolicited recruiter message is fraudulent.

## Common mistakes

- Replacing a long work history with a vague “aspiring analyst” label and no proof of relevant work.
- Hiding a previous career instead of showing the transferable work and explaining the change.
- Claiming data tools, impact or results that cannot be demonstrated in an interview.
- Copying generic “passionate, results-driven team player” language without an example.
- Stuffing the headline or skills list with keywords rather than describing actual ability.
- Treating a headshot, banner, connection count or profile-view count as evidence of job readiness.
- Treating older or undated LinkedIn advice as current without checking the feature. Skill assessments are no longer available [17]; one LinkedIn Talent Solutions article's 50-skill limit conflicts with the current Help page's 100-skill limit [7][21]. The current Help page should take precedence.
- Making unsubstantiated claims about recruiter search ranking or implying that profile changes guarantee discovery.
- Publishing a project or personal details without checking whether they are safe to share.

- What do West Midlands hiring managers want to see from a career changer with no paid analyst experience?
- Which LinkedIn Recruiter filters do local recruiters actually use for junior roles? Public LinkedIn product information confirms filters exist but does not settle local practice.
- How much should a learner disclose about a current job search if employed, given LinkedIn's privacy caveat?
- A first-person UK hiring-manager interview specific to entry-level data analysis was not located in the sources retrieved for this findings file; that is a gap, not evidence that hiring managers do not use LinkedIn.

## Checklist test

I do not have a personal LinkedIn profile or account, so I could not honestly test these boxes against my own live profile. I walked through the checklist as a prospective user against the available help documentation:

- **Easy:** the platform's stated controls for headline, About, Experience, skills, Featured work, public URL and Open to Work can be turned into concrete checks.
- **Hard:** translating a long career into concise evidence takes specific examples and care not to overstate tool use or outcomes; the checklist cannot supply a learner's personal evidence.
- **Unclear:** LinkedIn does not publish the exact recruiter-search ranking weights, and the checklist cannot establish which projects a particular local employer will value. The stale or conflicting help content on some features also means users should verify the current interface.

This is a checklist usability walk-through, not a personal-profile test.
