# SkillMatch Hackathon — My Project Development Diary

**Project:** SkillMatch  
**Hackathon:** Code Institute Data Analytics with AI, October–November 2026  
**Team:** Esmat, Phil, Oana, John and SK  
**Repository:** https://github.com/TidyWeb/SkillMatch  
**Website:** https://tidyweb.github.io/SkillMatch/

## 1. Before the hackathon: what I expected

Before hackathon week, Phil had briefly explained the idea to me. I knew I'd be working with him and Oana, and I liked the plan to build an app that would match live job adverts to the level of skills we had developed through Code Institute, particularly for entry-level roles. I was excited by the idea, but also a little nervous about how I could best contribute.

On Monday, our planning day, Phil and Oana explained the project to John and me in more detail. Phil had already put a lot of work into the app. We were asked to explore it, enter our profiles and think about the user experience. Getting into the app through an unfamiliar development environment was a new experience for me, but I managed it and could see what had already been built.

Through our discussions, the scope became clearer. It wasn't only going to be a job-matching app: we were also considering data analysis and guides to support graduates with LinkedIn and GitHub. John was keen to take the lead on the data analysis side, and I wanted to take ownership of something I could contribute directly. I decided to start with the LinkedIn guide and then help elsewhere as time allowed. By the end of Monday, knowing what I was responsible for made the project feel much more manageable.

## 2. What I worked on during the week

On Tuesday, Phil shared research prompts for the graduate guides. I used the LinkedIn prompt to investigate what makes an effective profile for someone changing career into an entry-level data analyst role. The aim was to rely on credible information, especially LinkedIn's own help pages, rather than generic lists of tips. I checked that the links worked, looked at how current the information was and compared findings from a few AI tools. The recommendations were broadly similar, so I continued with the original research.

I also looked at real LinkedIn profiles, including profiles I found after ChatGPT pointed me towards Code Institute success stories. I used AI to help put my observations into a comparison table. I was careful not to treat those profiles as proven examples of best practice: looking at a profile doesn't tell you how effective it is, or whether it has been kept up to date. One thing I noticed was that I couldn't always see a Featured section with project work, even though showing evidence of what you've done seemed useful to me.

Next, I tried the research checklist on my own LinkedIn profile. That brought up practical gaps I might otherwise have missed. For example, the Education section needed to be included, and adding a Featured section was less obvious than it sounded because of where LinkedIn places the option. I fed those findings into the guide content.

I used AI to produce a Markdown draft, then reviewed the content myself and went through it with Oana. After adding our feedback, I worked on an HTML version so the guide could be viewed as a web page. I used Phil's GitHub guide as a visual benchmark, experimented with output from different AI tools and kept refining the layout and instructions.

The images took more time than I expected. Screenshots from my account needed personal information removed, and covering or replacing that information made the results look messy. I eventually worked with AI to create illustrations based on the screenshots, then checked them repeatedly against the real interface so the labels, buttons and instructions made sense. Once I was happy with the guide, I shared it with the team. Phil used his AI agent to make further refinements, and I reviewed the revised version. We continued reviewing and refining the guides over the weekend. One of the details we were still checking was how LinkedIn's interface can appear differently in different environments.

I also reviewed Phil's GitHub guide. I liked the explanations of terms such as README, the screenshots showing where to click, and the examples of different profile layouts. In my first review, I asked for more practical guidance on how a beginner could actually build a profile like the examples. Phil fed that feedback into his AI agent to help develop another version of the guide.

On Friday, I reviewed a later version more thoroughly. I liked the larger, scrollable profile examples and the Markdown demonstrations showing both what you type and what appears on GitHub. My feedback focused on making it clearer whether a step related to the main profile, a profile README or a project README; explaining technical elements such as activity cards and linked images more simply; giving examples of what to write; and clarifying whether users should build their profiles as they worked through the guide or at the end. I approached the review from the perspective of someone learning to use GitHub, with a focus on whether a reader could follow the instructions and understand what to do.

As I had more time to explore other parts of SkillMatch, I also gave feedback on the app. On Friday, for example, a job requiring Czech appeared as a strong match for my profile, although I hadn't been asked about my language skills. I flagged my strong match to the team, and Phil recognised that language requirements needed to be considered in the filtering.

## 3. Challenges and how I worked through them

A hackathon week moves quickly, and there was a lot happening across different parts of the project. The team brought different strengths and levels of experience, and I was learning some of the tools and workflows while using them. Having a clear responsibility for the LinkedIn guide helped me stay focused while still contributing feedback elsewhere.

I found that AI-assisted work could take longer than it first appeared. Producing a draft was often quick, but checking the details, correcting images, making instructions accurate and reviewing new versions took repeated attempts. The screenshot problem was a good example of that. Getting an output that looked right wasn't enough; it also needed to be useful and reflect what someone would actually see.

Balancing meetings with time to get work done was another challenge. We had useful group discussions, but with such a short timeframe I sometimes wanted more uninterrupted time to work. Smaller, focused conversations could be especially productive. I appreciated Oana staying behind to help me create a GitHub branch and push my research, using Copilot to guide the process. She also made time to go through the Markdown guide with me and give feedback.

Our work went through quite a few versions and rounds of review. That reinforced the value of keeping track of changes and checking the latest output rather than assuming every revision was an improvement. As the week went on, I became more familiar with the project, and I was able to pay closer attention to the details in both my guide and the app.

## 4. How my ideas changed, and what I'd try differently

At first, I understood SkillMatch mainly as an app for matching graduates to entry-level job adverts. Over the week it developed into more of a package: the job-matching app, LinkedIn and GitHub guides, a CV Tailor and a data analysis section. SK joined the team during the week and contributed to the ideas and work as the project developed. The CV Tailor was one of the later additions, and I was particularly interested in how it connected a selected job advert with a person's own CV information. I explored the CV Tailor setup and biography questions, and how the feature was intended to work with selected job adverts.

I don't think there's one big decision I'd definitely change. You have to work through a task to discover which parts will take longer than expected. We took a flexible approach, which allowed people to help across different areas, and I valued that. One thing I'd be interested in trying on another short project is setting a few clearer end-of-day targets while keeping the flexibility to respond to new ideas and problems. I don't think there's necessarily one right way to do it.

## 5. How I used AI

AI was involved in several stages of my work: researching the LinkedIn guide, comparing sources, organising observations, drafting Markdown, converting it to HTML, creating visual examples and refining the result. I also used GitHub Copilot in VS Code as more than a typing assistant for the first time. I asked it questions and used its agent capabilities to help with files and GitHub tasks.

One of my biggest 'wow' moments was seeing the Markdown guide become an actual web page. I hadn't realised how accessible that process could be with AI. At the same time, the work showed how much attention the output still needs. I already knew from previous experience that AI results have to be checked, and I continued to check both the information and how it appeared in the finished guide. I also found that different AI tools were more useful for different tasks; Gemini, for example, didn't work as well for what I needed in this process.

I was impressed by the different AI agents Phil had set up for particular tasks. Our review process had a human element: versions came back to the team, we gave feedback, and that feedback informed the next revision. It made me interested in learning more about how specialised agents are built and how their output is validated in other workflows too.

## 6. What I've taken from the experience

I feel more confident about creating web content with AI. I know it takes careful prompting, checking and revision, but I now have practical experience of turning research and Markdown into a guide people can actually view online. I've also had useful practice with branches, pushing work and pull requests on GitHub, even though I still have more to learn about collaborative workflows.

The teamwork is one of the things I'll remember most. To me, working as a team isn't just about giving every task to whoever is already best at it. It's also about everyone having a chance to contribute, use their skills, learn and help one another. I appreciated being able to focus on the LinkedIn guide while also reviewing the GitHub guide and giving feedback on the app. I was genuinely impressed by what the team managed to put together in such a short time.

I want to keep building on the things I practised here: using GitHub collaboratively, developing web content, working with AI tools more effectively and testing outputs carefully. The hackathon gave me a much clearer sense of how those skills can come together in a shared project.
